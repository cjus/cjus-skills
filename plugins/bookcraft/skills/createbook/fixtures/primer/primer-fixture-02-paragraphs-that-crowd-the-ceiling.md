# Paragraphs That Crowd the Ceiling

Every paragraph in this chapter runs close to the ceiling on purpose, because that is the pattern the clustering report exists to catch. A writer handed a limit tends to treat it as the size a paragraph should be, so the paragraphs come out as uniform blocks just under the line, and a reader notices the sameness before noticing anything the paragraphs say. The report counts how many paragraphs in a chapter reach most of the limit and speaks up when that share is high.

## Why a limit becomes a target

A limit tells a writer how far they may go, and nothing tells them how far they should go, so the limit is the only number in view. Under deadline, a writer checks each paragraph against that number and stops when it is close, and the result is a chapter where almost every paragraph lands within a few words of the limit. Nobody decided that a paragraph ought to be that long. The size came from the only measurement anyone was looking at while they wrote.

<!-- src: fill -->

The same thing happens to a word count for a whole chapter. A chapter given a ceiling of two thousand words, and then asked by a reviewer to explain three more things, grows until it sits just under two thousand, whatever the subject needed. Each added explanation was reasonable on its own, and no single step looked like padding, yet the chapter ended where the ceiling was rather than where its explanation finished. That is the same failure at a larger scale, and it is harder to see from inside.

<!-- src: fill -->

## What the report measures

The report reads every prose paragraph in a chapter and counts the ones that run to eighty-five percent of the limit or more. Under the primer profile that is eighty-five words of the hundred, and under the guide and narration profiles it is seventy-six of the ninety. When two in every five paragraphs reach that mark, in a chapter with at least eight paragraphs, the report names the chapter and the count, so a person can look at the chapter and judge it for themselves.

<!-- src: fill -->

It never fails the run, because a chapter can have good reasons for long paragraphs, and a script cannot tell a dense reference passage from prose written to a number. What it can do is make the pattern visible. A person reading the report then opens the chapter, reads a few of the paragraphs it names, and decides whether they were written to the thought or to the limit, which only a reader can judge.

<!-- src: fill -->

## Why eight paragraphs

A chapter with three paragraphs could have all three near the limit by chance, and a report on it would say nothing useful. Eight paragraphs is enough for a pattern to mean something, while still being small enough that a short chapter written to the limit gets caught. The number is a judgment rather than a measurement, and it can move if the report proves too eager or too quiet on real books, which is how the other thresholds in this checker were set over time.

<!-- src: fill -->

The share was chosen the same way. Two books written under the old ninety-word stop had sixty-three and thirty-nine percent of their paragraphs within ten words of it, and a short book written under a ceiling had four percent. Forty percent sits between them, low enough to catch the first book and the worst chapters of the second, and high enough that the third book, whose paragraphs varied with their content, stays clear of it in every chapter.

<!-- src: fill -->

## What to do when it fires

The fix is not to trim every paragraph by ten words, which only moves the cluster lower. It is to let each paragraph be the size of its thought. Some ideas need one sentence, and a single sentence standing alone often lands harder than a full block does. Others need a long paragraph with an example inside it. A chapter that mixes the two reads as written by someone thinking about the reader rather than about the limit in front of them, and that difference shows.
